# Top War: Battle Game

## 1. Overview

- **Genre:** Sci-fi/military base-building strategy ("4X-lite"/SLG) with a
  merge-to-upgrade mechanic layered on top. Marketed with the tagline "Build,
  Merge, Battle."
- **Developer:** Topwar Studio (Beijing River Games Co., Ltd.)
- **Publisher:** RiverGame Limited / River Game HK Limited (Hong Kong)
- **Release date:** Soft-launched on Android August 8, 2019; full iOS/Android
  release December 31, 2019.
- **Platforms:** iOS and Android (portrait mobile). No native PC release; played
  on PC only via Android emulators (BlueStacks, LDPlayer — both publish official
  guide content for it, implying meaningful emulator playership).
- **Scale:** Sources disagree by a wide margin depending on vintage/
  methodology. An early Apptopia writeup cites ~40M lifetime downloads and ~$83M
  lifetime IAP revenue at that point, with 421% YoY IAP revenue growth and 531%
  YoY DAU growth, and 90-day download volume (19M) already surpassing Clash of
  Clans and Lords Mobile in that window. A later, SensorTower-derived readout
  puts lifetime downloads around 78-180M and lifetime revenue around $1.1B. A
  2026 industry report (Gamigion, covering publisher RiverGame's $2B lifetime
  user spending) says Top War still accounts for 83% of RiverGame's total
  revenue but is in decline, running about
  $8M/month — well behind RiverGame's newer title Top Heroes: Kingdom
  Saga ($20M/month roughly a year after launch). Read as: a breakout that became
  one of the highest-grossing SLGs of its cohort, now a mature/ declining cash
  cow being outpaced by its own publisher's newer title.
- **App store standing:** Apple listing shows 4.1/5 from ~102K ratings (13+ age
  rating, ~1.2GB install); a separate aggregator reported Google Play at 4.6/5
  from ~777K ratings. Either way, a large, positive-leaning review base.
- **Monetization model:** Free-to-play with in-app purchases spanning $0.99 to
  $99.99 "Hot Sale Packs," a paid ad-removal subscription (reported around
  $30/month by one reviewer — notably expensive for the genre), rewarded video
  ads for currency/progression boosts (called out as unusually generous for a
  genre that's normally light on player-elective ads), gacha hero recruitment,
  loot boxes, a VIP level system (VIP 16, the top tier, reportedly requires
  roughly $60,000 of lifetime spend), and recurring limited-time
  packs/battle-pass-style co-op event tracks.

## 2. Core gameplay loop

The distinguishing hook of Top War is that **merge replaces the wait-timer** as
the primary upgrade action across almost every system: buildings, troops, and
hero-adjacent items are all upgraded by dragging one item onto an identical one,
producing an instantly-available higher-tier item rather than starting a
multi-hour construction timer. A level-4 gold mine merged with another level-4
gold mine becomes a level-5 mine immediately, not after a countdown; two level-8
troops merged become a level-9 troop squad. Guides describe the payoff curve as
steep — one guide cites a level-5 Gold Mine paying out "almost 7 times" a
level-3 mine's output for only ~30% more resource cost, meaning the incentive to
keep merging rather than sit on low-tier duplicates is constant and visible.

A typical session loop:

1. **Collect** — pull accumulated resources out of mines/harvesters (which cap
   out and stop producing until manually redeemed).
2. **Merge** — drag-and-combine duplicate buildings, troop stacks, and (in some
   systems) tech/component items to push them up tiers. Presented as a
   puzzle-like, tactile action rather than a menu purchase, and the game's
   most-repeated action per session.
3. **Build/place** — place newly-merged or newly-unlocked buildings on the
   island grid; expand the buildable footprint as the base grows.
4. **Send troops out** — attack NPCs/Dark Legion forces on the surrounding map
   for materials and tech chests, or engage other players' bases/rallies for PvP
   resources and standing.
5. **Check quests/events** — daily quests, alliance quests, and time-boxed
   events (gacha banners, card-flip challenges) that reward the currencies
   merging and building consume.
6. **AFK/idle resolution** — combat itself is largely automated/non-tactical
   ("AFK battles" in one reviewer's words); the player's strategic input is in
   composition (which units/heroes to field) and timing (when to rally, when to
   defend), not real-time control.

Because merging is instant, perceived pacing is much faster than a typical
timer-driven SLG in short sessions — there's always a productive action
available even when nothing is actively "cooking," which several sources single
out as the addictive core of the early game.

## 3. Base building & progression systems

- **Base:** Players start on "a barren, deserted island" and build outward,
  placing buildings on a grid to train armies and unlock territory. Buildings
  (mines, barracks, shipyard/naval base, etc.) are leveled primarily via merging
  duplicate instances rather than a simple spend-and-wait upgrade.
- **Merge level caps are gated by research, not raw merging:** a building can't
  be merged past a level unless the matching tech has been researched first —
  e.g., raising "Naval Base Combination" level to 7 in the tech tree is a
  prerequisite for merging (producing) a level-7 Naval Base at all. This ties
  the merge mechanic directly into a conventional tech-tree gate rather than
  letting merging alone be an escape valve from progression pacing.
- **Tech/research system:** the "Intel Command Center" (later "Element Lab" at
  higher tiers) is the main research building. Its own level is raised with gold
  and "General R&D Plans." Basic Research has three parallel tracks that must
  each reach a threshold level (e.g., level 10) before a subsequent tier (Basic
  Research 11) unlocks, gated additionally on Element Lab level — a fairly deep,
  branching tech tree by mid-to-late game.
- **Hero system:** heroes are the RPG/collection layer bolted onto the
  base-building shell (per GameRefinery's analysis, an unusual choice for a
  4X-style game, which more commonly emphasizes anonymous unit counts over named
  characters). Heroes have bios, skills, and equipment; rated in three rarity
  tiers — R (blue), SR (purple), SSR (gold) — and belong to one of three
  military types (Army/Land, Navy, Air Force), matching the troop classes. Each
  hero has three base stats: War (unit attack), DEF (unit HP), and CMD/Command
  (march/troop size the hero can lead) — higher rarity heroes lead larger troop
  counts, directly scaling combat power. Heroes are acquired via gacha
  "recruitment," leveled with experience books, and promoted (star-upgraded)
  with hero shards from events, recruitment, and a dedicated "orange shard"
  economy at the high end — one guide estimates roughly 1,320 orange shards to
  push three heroes from 1-star to 5-star, typically paced across multiple
  events rather than bought at once.
- **Power/CP-adjacent systems:** the game doesn't foreground a single visible
  "Power" number in the sources gathered, but overall strength is a composite of
  base/building level, merged-troop tier, hero rarity/star level/gear
  ("components"), and VIP level — all of which are cross-referenced in alliance
  rally-leader selection ("the whale with the highest single-march power leads
  the rally").
- **Profession/class choice:** at level 45, players pick one of two
  specialization trees — **Combat Elite** (favors spenders: combat bonuses,
  larger rallies) or **Mechanical Master** (favors F2P players: gathering speed
  and resource-collection bonuses, plus can build "super" resource buildings
  alliance members can gather from). Both reportedly offer equivalent
  unit-damage-reduction/HP bonuses, so the choice is about playstyle emphasis
  rather than a strictly worse F2P option. Advanced guides describe a late-game
  respec pattern: rush Combat Elite first using speedups, then transition into a
  "Tactical Master" branch for PvP.

## 4. Resource economy

- **Core resources:** gold, food, and oil are named as the main economic
  resources; guides advise prioritizing food/oil gathering since "gold will come
  to you naturally" from base production.
- **Production/storage:** resource buildings (e.g., gold mines) generate output
  continuously into an attached harvester/storage buffer, which has a hard cap —
  production **stops** once the harvester is full until the player manually
  redeems it, incentivizing regular check-ins rather than pure idle accrual.
  Higher building level increases both the harvester's capacity and the
  per-collection yield. One guide cites a level-something gold mine yielding
  roughly 600k coins/hour as a rough benchmark.
- **Secondary "resources":** tech/R&D chests and gold chests act as gated
  progression currencies layered on top of the raw resources — one advanced
  guide flags tech chests as the typical early-game bottleneck, with R&D demand
  increasing sharply around levels 70-80, and cites needing roughly 20,000 gold
  chests worth of accumulation to unlock additional docks at level 80 — i.e.,
  resource sinks scale up substantially as the game matures, well past what
  casual daily collection can sustain.
- **Premium currencies:** gems (premium currency) and coins are the two named
  main currencies; gems are earned through quests/events or purchased directly,
  and spent on speedups, recruitment, and shop purchases.
- **Spend sinks:** merging (consumes duplicate items, which themselves cost
  resources to produce/train), building placement/upgrades, hero
  leveling/promotion (experience books, shards), tech/R&D upgrades, troop
  training, and event-specific currencies (gacha tickets, card-flip challenges).
  Vitality (a stamina-like resource, restored partly via a specific hero, Diana)
  gates how much PvE grinding (hitting Dark Forces, world-boss-style targets) a
  player can do per day.

## 5. Combat systems

- **Resolution model:** battles are largely automated/simulated rather than
  directly controlled — one long-form player account contrasts Top War's "AFK
  battles" unfavorably against genuinely tactical combat. GameRefinery notes
  combat resolves as single composite entities (a hero + its led troops) per
  side rather than large-scale unit-by-unit simulation, which simplifies the
  underlying math despite the large army visuals.
- **PvE:** an overworld/island map populated by NPC enemies (used for
  materials/tech chests), a "Dark Legion" faction as the game's standing
  antagonist (also called "Dark Forces" — Army, Navy, and Air Force enemy
  variants, with higher-level Dark Forces worth more event points), and a
  "Sandtable"-based world-boss/stationary-target system with rotating
  Sandtable/Warzone "exercises."
- **PvP — ladder/world map:** "Warzone" is described as a PvP ladder with
  rotating scenarios, both in-server and cross-server (SVS — Server vs. Server).
  Direct base attacks and rallying other players' territory are part of ordinary
  world-map play once past the early NPC-focused phase (guides explicitly tell
  new players to build up before engaging PvP). A cited fairness complaint:
  relocating your base after being attacked breaks the attacker's ability to
  retaliate, since the attack record is tied to the coordinates at the time of
  the original attack, not your current position.
- **PvP — Capital Throne (weekly alliance event):** a one-hour, server-wide
  contest to control a central "Capitol." Hold-time requirements shrink across
  four windows (30 min hold in the first 30 minutes, 15 min in the 30-60 minute
  window, 5 min in the 60-90 minute window, then simple possession-at- timeout
  after that) — deliberately opening the door for a mid-tier alliance to snipe a
  win late even if it can't out-brawl the server's strongest alliance early. Two
  neutral Silos on the map are a secondary lever: an alliance holding a Silo
  launches missiles at the Capitol every two minutes, damaging whoever is
  garrisoned there, so Silo control matters even without a direct Capitol
  assault. The event leader gets +15% ATK and +15% HP server-wide (for their
  alliance) plus a march-priority bonus, and assigns 8 officer buff-granting
  positions (see § 6).
- **Merge tiers and combat power:** merged unit tier directly scales combat
  output (higher-tier merged troops "deal more damage and may strike multiple
  enemies per turn," per one guide), and merge level itself is gated behind tech
  research, so raw merging alone can't outrun the tech tree — the two systems
  are coupled rather than the merge mechanic being a pure shortcut. Community
  tier lists (Pocket Gamer, Packsify) rank heroes by rarity/
  exclusive-skill-level combination rather than base stats alone, and describe
  named "metas" per troop-type (a "Magnetic Pulse" tank meta built around hero
  roles — Opener/Stacker/Detonator/Anchor — and an "Ignition" meta for Air
  Force), indicating combat balance is heavily hero-skill-driven at the high
  end, not just raw merged-unit tier.

## 6. Alliance/social systems

- **Alliances** are presented as close to mandatory for meaningful progress —
  guides uniformly recommend joining one early to reduce hostile pressure and
  unlock cooperative quests/shared gathering (e.g., Mechanical Master players
  can build "super" resource buildings other alliance members gather from).
- **Officer structure:** the Capital Throne event exposes a formal 8-role
  officer system an alliance leader assigns: Staff Member, Army Commander, Naval
  Commander, Air Force Commander, Military Governor, Economy Minister, Tech
  Expert, and Goddess of War — each granting a different member-wide buff.
  Likely reflective of the alliance's general permission/role structure rather
  than an event-only construct, though sources don't confirm that generalization
  directly.
- **Coordination culture:** large events reward advance planning — a rally
  leader chosen by highest single-march power, same-troop-type rally composition
  for damage efficiency, pre-positioning bases near the Capitol, and members
  budgeting resources/speedups weeks ahead. Alliances that skip a weekly
  "recovery budget" reportedly fall out of competitive participation by week
  3-4.
- **Cross-alliance dynamics:** SVS (Server vs. Server) events imply a
  server-level "us vs. them" framing beyond individual alliances; specific
  cross-alliance diplomacy tooling (formal treaties, non-aggression pacts, etc.)
  was not found in the sources gathered — plausible but unconfirmed.
- **Chat/gifting:** not well documented in the sources found; guides reference
  "helping each other" and shared gathering/resource buildings as alliance-level
  cooperation, but explicit chat features or a gifting system (sending
  resources/items directly to other players) weren't confirmed in any source
  consulted. Flagging this as a real gap rather than guessing.

## 7. Additional/side features

- **Collaboration/crossover events:** Top War has run high-profile IP crossovers
  with Transformers — an initial event around August 2021, and a larger sequel
  event ("Darkness Falls," announced for a following March) where Autobots and
  Decepticons (Optimus Prime, Megatron, Arcee, Soundwave + Laserbeak, Autobot
  Blaster + Ramhorn) form a temporary alliance with Top War's factions against a
  shared enemy. These crossovers add collectible named heroes (Optimus Prime is
  an S-tier Army hero in current tier lists) and are explicitly called out by
  PocketGamer.biz as breaking prior performance records for the game — i.e., a
  meaningful revenue/engagement driver, not just flavor.
- **Recurring gacha/collection events:** named events like an "Acadia" gacha and
  "Flippy" card-flip challenges recur on a schedule per GameRefinery's analysis,
  layering limited-time collection loops on top of the permanent
  hero-recruitment gacha.
- **Battle-pass-style system:** implemented as a cooperative task track called
  "Alliance Transport Team" rather than a traditional solo battle pass — tasks
  appear to be alliance-cooperative rather than purely individual, per
  GameRefinery.
- **Rewarded video ads:** used as an active currency/progression-boost source
  players opt into, which GameRefinery calls out as unusual for the genre (most
  SLGs are light on player-facing ad monetization).
- **Side PvE modes:** "Hunting Guild" (quest-based treasure-guard farming for
  rewards, including a "wheel" reward structure), "Dimensional Mine" attacks,
  and "Island Battle" progression are named as additional daily-activity systems
  layered around the core base/merge/PvP loop.

## 8. Early game (first session / first few days)

Multiple sources converge on the early game being unusually compelling
specifically because of the merge loop's instant-gratification pacing:

- One reviewer described downloading the game "on a whim" and "ended up playing
  for hours straight" the first session; another said "I downloaded it... to my
  surprise it is super engaging"; a third called the early impression
  deceptively slow but noted "once you level up your base... it['s] so fun."
- The mechanical reason cited repeatedly: because merging resolves instantly (no
  wait timer), there is _always_ something actionable to do — a duplicate pair
  to merge — keeping the moment-to-moment loop dense from the first minutes,
  unlike timer-gated SLGs where a new player quickly hits a "come back in 4
  hours" wall.
- Early progression is guided by main-quest chapters that hand out gold and
  upgrade materials for objectives, nudging players toward specific
  buildings/upgrades rather than leaving base layout fully open-ended.
- Guides consistently tell new players to prioritize mines first, then
  troops/barracks; merge everything mergeable rather than hoard duplicates; and
  stay on NPC/PvE content until base and army are established before engaging
  other players — PvP exposure is paced behind a soft power threshold rather
  than opened immediately.
- Joining an alliance is recommended almost immediately, since it meaningfully
  reduces hostile pressure from other players even at low levels.
- The recurring negative counterpoint even here: reviewers report the ads depict
  a different, more tactical battle experience than what's delivered ("If the
  game was fun[ny] as the promotional videos show..."; "EVERY ad you see on this
  game is FAKE! All you do is merge people and click") — a first-session
  expectation mismatch and recurring churn risk, despite the loop testing well
  for people who stick around past that disappointment.

## 9. Mid game

- Guides describe the game "becom[ing] harder to level up after a certain stage"
  — a soft difficulty/pacing wall rather than a hard gate, where continued
  progress leans more on sustained daily engagement (quests, event cycles,
  alliance cooperation) than on any single mechanic.
- The merge mechanic does **not** fade into the background mechanically — it
  remains the literal upgrade method for buildings and troops throughout the
  game (merge levels are still being unlocked via tech research well into the
  70-80 level range per advanced guides) — but its _feel_ shifts: where early
  merging is opportunistic and constant, mid-to-late merging becomes something
  to plan around resource/tech-chest bottlenecks (tech chests are explicitly
  named as the early-to-mid bottleneck), a spontaneous action turned budgeted
  one. Attention shifts toward the level-45 profession choice (Combat Elite vs.
  Mechanical Master) and hero investment (shards, skill levels,
  equipment/"components") as the more strategically active layer, with merge
  itself becoming closer to upkeep than star attraction.
- Daily-activity checklists proliferate in mid-game guides (Dimensional Mine
  attacks, Warzone PvP, Sandtable exercises, Island Battle, Hunting Guild) — the
  loop broadens from "build and merge" into "clear a daily checklist across
  several parallel systems," a common SLG mid-game shape.
- Alliance-scale events (Capital Throne weekly, SVS) become the primary
  organizing structure for a player's week rather than solo base growth alone.

## 10. Late/end game

- **Whale-driven top end:** VIP 16, the top VIP tier, reportedly requires
  roughly $60,000 of cumulative spend, and guides note "there are more VIP 16
  players than you would think" — a real, non-trivial population of extreme
  spenders at the top of the ladder.
- **Endgame content is heavily hero/component-optimization-driven:** advanced
  guides describe pushing 3 "meta" heroes from 1-star to 5-star as a structured,
  multi-event-cycle project (~1,320 orange shards for three heroes),
  prioritizing exclusive-skill levels before star-ups, and timing purchases
  around reward-multiplier events (named examples: "Power Drill," "Blessing
  Clover") rather than spending reactively.
- **Tactical Master / PvP respec:** late-game guides describe a two-phase
  profession strategy — rush Combat Elite early using speedups, then transition
  into a PvP-focused "Tactical Master" branch — suggesting end-game depth is
  more about sequencing/build planning than new mechanical content.
- **Alliance warfare as the main end-game activity:** Capital Throne (weekly)
  and SVS are the primary structured competitive content once personal base
  growth plateaus; alliances that skip a weekly resource budget for Capital
  Throne fall out of competitive contention within a few weeks — a classic
  whale-and-log-in treadmill shape.
- **Revenue decline signal:** industry data (2026) shows Top War's monthly
  revenue has fallen to ~$8M/month, well below its historical peak and its own
  publisher's newer title — consistent with an end-game that has calcified into
  a spend-and-optimize loop for a shrinking veteran population rather than one
  still generating fresh late-game content for a broad base.

## 11. Community sentiment

Coverage of this game's community (App Store/Play reviews, YouTube/press
writeups, and secondary discussion of Reddit reaction) converges on one very
consistent split: **the merge loop itself is well-liked; the marketing and the
F2P pacing wall are the recurring complaints.** Direct Reddit access was
unavailable in this research session — both the web-search and web-fetch tools
could not reach reddit.com at all, so no r/TopWar threads were read firsthand;
treat this section as thinner on Reddit-specific data than ideal.

**Praise** (App Store/Play reviews, aggregated by third-party review sites):

- "Love this game. I love how many aspects there are to the game. I love the
  community and the building of my armies."
- "it has lots of amazing things to do and plus it's free you get to build merge
  and battle"
- "Got on a whim and ended up playing for hours straight."
- "The start seems boring but once you level up your base... it['s] so fun."
- "I like that there isn't any ads in the game" / "It is amazing and there [are]
  almost no ads" — several reviewers praise low forced-ad pressure, notable
  since many F2P mobile games lean much harder on interstitial ads.
- Pocket Gamer's own editorial framing calls the game "super-fun and engaging,"
  offering "more... than similar games like Clash of Clans" and that it "made
  them see base builder games from a whole new perspective" — an unusually
  strong endorsement from a press outlet for this genre.

**Recurring complaints:**

- **Misleading advertising is the single most repeated complaint across every
  source type** (App Store reviews, a personal blog post, Apptopia's analysis).
  Ads depict tactical, hands-on battle gameplay that doesn't match the actual
  merge-and-mostly-automated-battle experience. Quotes: "If the game was fun[ny]
  as the promotional videos show[s], this would be a good game. But no, it is
  boring, repetitive, poorly finished"; "EVERY add [ad] you see on this game is
  FAKE! All you do is merge people and click." A long-form blog post ("My
  Destructive Parasocial Relationship with TopWar: BattleGame") frames this as
  the game's real hook being the ads themselves, not the app — the author found
  the actual gameplay uninteresting ("Tactical battles interest me. AFK battles,
  not so much") and lost interest before ever hitting a paywall, but was
  fascinated by how divorced the marketing was from the product ("we can't show
  how the game actually plays, because nobody would download it").
- **Pay-to-win pressure and steep late-progression cost.** Multiple sources
  describe the game as "completely pay to win," citing the ability to reach rank
  60 in a single day given enough spend, and that competitive PvP standing
  against paying players becomes very difficult without spending. AppsPirate's
  review flags the ad-removal subscription as expensive (~$30/month) relative to
  genre norms.
- **Billing/support issues.** Some reviewers reported being billed twice for the
  same package, accounts frozen after disputing a charge, and one described
  losing 10+ months of progress unexpectedly ("Imagine working hard to get as
  far as you can in a game for 10+ months, only to log in one day and have it
  all gone") — a trust/support complaint distinct from pay-to-win.
- **Combat resolution feels unsatisfying/opaque to some players** — e.g. an
  account of 100 level-79 tanks losing to 10 level-80 tanks because the game
  only fields 10 units per side per combat round regardless of total army size,
  reading as counterintuitive to players expecting raw numeric superiority to
  matter more directly.
- **Location-based attack exploits** (see § 5) also surface as a community
  fairness complaint, not just a mechanical footnote.
- **Data/storage weight.** One review aggregation notes the game reportedly
  consumes 30-40GB of data, unusually heavy for a mobile title.

Overall shape: a game whose core merge-driven loop is genuinely well-regarded by
people who play past the first session (strong organic ratings, enthusiastic
reviews about the base-building/merge tactility), but whose acquisition
marketing and monetization aggressiveness generate an unusually loud, consistent
stream of complaints — the "advertised game vs. actual game" gap is the single
most-repeated criticism across every source type consulted.
